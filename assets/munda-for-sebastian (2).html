The model, in your words.

Seb's field school
One farmer hosts. Two plots on their land — same size, same seed, same spacing, same everything except one variable. The host farmer does all the work, not the group. The group observes.

Three meetings a year. Pre-season to plan what to test. Mid-season to observe when work pressure drops. Post-season to weigh, compare, and digest.

25 groups running already — testing manure, fertiliser, varieties, intercropping, conservation agriculture, rotations. Each one chosen by the farmer group based on their own priorities.

This is radically simpler than the FAO model. No group plot. No weekly sessions. No facilitator training that takes a season. One farmer, their own field, three days of everyone's time. The cost is essentially zero.

The comparison plot does the teaching. If the facilitator gets something slightly wrong, the side-by-side result corrects it. The method is self-correcting — that's what makes it replicable by anyone.

On facilitators
Need to get the basics right to avoid error. All things the same, apart from one difference, makes the lesson. Same size plots, spacing, variety. Then change one variable — e.g. with and without manure.

Facilitator needs to understand the logic. Always best to see it working to grasp the process. But really, that's the trick we're still working on — where to place emphasis and how. In theory, anyone can be a facilitator.

The core facilitator skill is one thing: keep the comparison clean. Same everything except one variable. If they hold that discipline, the field does the rest.

On what's evolving
Many things. Mainly crop green manure production using velvet bean and sun hemp. Also strip intercropping and mixed intercropping options for maize and legumes — maximising synergy, LER and productivity. Still a work in progress, but very promising.

This confirms the knowledge base can't be static. What Sebastian recommends today may change next season. A printed manual is dead on arrival. The digital tool evolves with him.

The multiplication model.

You train trainers. They train facilitators. Facilitators host field schools. Best farmers from each season become next season's facilitators.

One workshop from you → 25 trainers → 750 facilitators → 18,750 farmers. Run several workshops a year — as many as demand and energy allow. You're the one in the room. You're the one walking the plots. You're the quality eye that keeps the methodology clean as it cascades.

Your role isn't minimised. It's redirected — from working with 25 farmers to shaping how thousands learn.

The data nobody is collecting.

The question we didn't ask
How the information these groups produce can be shared with the wider community and at a national level. Using these farmer-led experimental plots as data for informing research, education and policy. Tens of thousands of data points created every year — how do we use that to the advantage of our rural and national development?

This is bigger than extension. If 750 facilitators each run one FFS per season, that's 750 controlled comparisons — real yield data, from real fields, under real conditions, across multiple districts and soil types. Structured and aggregated, that's an agricultural research dataset built from the bottom up. No research station. No million-dollar project. Farmers producing the evidence that informs national policy.

The app makes this possible. Not as an advice tool — as a data capture tool. The facilitator enters: practice tested, plot sizes, yields, soil type, zone, rainfall estimate, conclusion. Structured, tagged, comparable across thousands of sites.

Where this goes.

Seb's five-year picture
Public and NGO staff employing the process as part of their core extension system and programming. Each cooperative running at least one FFS group to answer pressing issues surrounding agricultural systems. Inclusion of this process in curriculum for colleges and higher education.

That's institutional adoption. Extension services, cooperatives, and agricultural colleges all using the same lightweight methodology. With structured data flowing from every group into a national evidence base. That's the vision we're building toward.

Challenges. Honest ones.

1 · AIZ Regulation
Only trained and certified staff can advise farmers — including through videos, social media, and digital tools. The Munda app, the videos, the entire cascade has a legal dimension. If a non-certified facilitator uses the tool to guide a farmer, that may be non-compliant. This is the gate. Everything else depends on resolving this.

Two possible paths: position Munda as a facilitation support tool (not advisory), which may fall outside AIZ scope. Or get the FFS approach itself submitted as a "released technology" through the extension committee. Both need investigation.

2 · Methodology degradation
The FFS model is simple. But "simple" degrades across levels of multiplication. By the time a facilitator three steps removed from you sets up a comparison, they might allow two variables to change, skip the weighing, or turn the observation into a lecture. You named this yourself — existing field schools are "top-down demonstrations in essence." The risk is that scaling turns your approach back into the thing it was designed to replace.

Mitigation: the app enforces methodology at the point of capture. The data form only accepts one variable per comparison. It requires yield entries for both plots. It won't let a facilitator skip the post-harvest review. The tool becomes the guardrail.
3 · Existing FFS reputation
Field schools already exist in Zambia. Many are heavy, group-run, top-down, and don't work well. Your model is fundamentally different — but anyone hearing "Farmer Field School" may think of the old version. The naming and positioning has to make the distinction clear. This is not what they've seen before.

4 · Data quality
Farmer-led experiments are only useful as data if the collection is consistent. A farmer writing "the manure plot did better" is an anecdote. A structured entry with yields, soil type, zone, and single variable tagged is data. The app needs to make structured capture easy enough that facilitators actually do it — on a phone, in the field, in under five minutes.
5 · Sebastian as single point of knowledge
The practices keep evolving. The knowledge base needs updating. The quality of the cascade depends on the quality of the source. If something happens to you, or if you simply can't keep up with the demand, the system stalls. The AI tool partially solves this — your thinking is captured and accessible. But the evolving edge of the knowledge still lives in your head.

One take. Your farm. An actual FFS group doing a post-harvest review. Real, not scripted. Show the two plots, the weighing, the conversation, the conclusion. 3–5 minutes. This is the recruitment tool for the first Train-the-Trainer workshop.

The 10–15 facilitators each run one FFS. Structured data captured through the app. Seb visits a sample to quality-check methodology. Real yield comparisons from farmer-led trials. Collected through a digital system. Demonstrating a scalable extension methodology that doesn't depend on subsidised inputs. That's the brief for the MoA familiarisation exercise and CATSP engagement.
