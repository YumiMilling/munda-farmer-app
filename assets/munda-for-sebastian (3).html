Seb, everything at Kafue works. The yields are verified. The communities at Mpanshya speak for themselves. Twenty years of proof.

The thing that doesn't work yet is getting it out of your head and into a thousand fields at once.

Working with a few farmer groups a year — that's maybe 100 people. Zambia has over a million farming households. You already know this. You've been asking the same question: how to spread it.

Here's what we think the answer looks like. Tell us where we're wrong.

You stop working with farmer groups.

Not because that work doesn't matter — it does. But because every hour you spend with one group of 25 is an hour you're not training someone who could reach 25 groups.

You train trainers. They train facilitators. The facilitators run the field schools. You've said the practices are simple enough that anyone can do it and replicate it. So the job isn't teaching farmers — it's teaching the people who'll teach the people who work with farmers.

One workshop from you reaches 25 trainers. Each trainer reaches 30 facilitators. Each facilitator runs a group of 25 farmers. That's nearly 19,000 farmers from a single workshop.

Run three workshops a year, and you're in every province.

This doesn't mean you sit at home.

You run the workshops. As many as you want — the more you run, the further it reaches. You're the one standing in front of the room because nobody else has your twenty years on the soil.

You travel to where facilitators are working. You walk their plots, watch how they're running sessions, see where the message is holding and where it's drifting. You're the quality eye. When you see something going wrong, you fix it — not by writing a memo, but by standing in a field and showing them.

You keep learning. Your farm is still the lab. Intercropping keeps evolving, you said. New combinations, new failures, new insights every season. That learning feeds back into everything — the training, the tool, the whole system.

You're the face and the voice. When a district agricultural coordinator asks "who's behind this?" — your name is the answer. Your results. Your farm.

The only thing that changes: you're not the bottleneck anymore. Not because you step back — because there are a thousand people carrying what you taught them into fields you'll never visit. And they're doing it right because the method corrects itself and the tool holds your thinking.

Not the FAO field school.

You said their model takes too much time. Season-long weekly meetings. Facilitator training that takes a whole season before you even start. Too heavy to cascade fast.

Your version is lighter because the practices are simpler. A comparison plot corrects mistakes the facilitator makes — the field teaches, not the person. That's what makes it replicable by anyone.

But we need you to define what "your version" actually looks like in a repeatable format.

What does your field school actually look like?
Not FAO's version. Yours. How many times does the group meet? What happens at each one? What's the bare minimum that works?

The digital tool.

You wondered whether AI could put this information into a reasonable format. It can. We've built a phone-based tool — Munda — that holds your principles, your failure modes, your "it depends on the context" thinking.

A facilitator in Pemba types: "Sandy soil, no cattle, Striga in the maize." The tool responds the way you would — not with a recipe, but with principles, trade-offs, and "try this small and see." It doesn't replace you. It makes your way of thinking available to every facilitator at 2am when you're asleep.

The practices keep evolving. That's why this is an app, not a book. You review it each season, update what's changed, and the tool evolves with you.
